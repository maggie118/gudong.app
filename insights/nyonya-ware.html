南洋最贵的那只碗

The most expensive bowl in Nanyang

它不在景德镇烧，也不全是中国画的

Not fired in Jingdezhen — and not entirely painted in China

1800s — 1930s
2026-03-092026-03-09 · 4 分钟阅读4 min read · 古董圈编辑部 · 未经授权禁止转载Gudong Editorial · No reposting without permission

如果你走进槟城或马六甲的峇峇娘惹老宅，会看到餐桌上一整套粉彩瓷器——凤凰、牡丹、红绿交错，浓艳得像新娘的嫁衣。

这就是娘惹瓷。

它不在景德镇烧，也不全是中国画的。它是南洋华人自己"造"出来的一种瓷器——从景德镇买白瓷，运到广东上色，再运回南洋使用。

这套流程，比你想的复杂。

I

先说说"娘惹"是谁

"娘惹"是马来语 Nyonya 的音译，指的是早期南洋华人移民与当地马来女性通婚后的女性后代。男性叫峇峇（Baba）。

这个群体从 15 世纪就开始形成——比英国人来到马来亚还早四百年。

他们既不是纯粹的中国人，也不是纯粹的马来人。他们有自己的语言、服饰、饮食和审美。而娘惹瓷，就是他们审美最集中的体现。

❦ ❦ ❦
II

为什么是粉彩

中国瓷器有很多种——青花、青瓷、单色釉、五彩。但娘惹瓷几乎只用一种：粉彩。

为什么？

因为粉彩最艳。

粉彩是一种在釉上彩绘的技术，可以调出柔和的红色、粉色、黄色、绿色。它不像青花那样只有蓝白两色，也不像单色釉那样素净。对生活在热带、喜欢色彩浓烈的峇峇娘惹家庭来说，粉彩是最合适的选择。

他们特别偏爱两种颜色：粉红和孔雀绿。前者代表喜庆，后者代表富贵。这两种颜色搭配在一起，就是最典型的娘惹瓷。

典型纹饰也很固定：凤凰、牡丹、八宝、蝙蝠、蝴蝶。都是中国传统吉祥图案，但配色和布局已经和中原不一样了——更满、更艳、更热闹。

娘惹瓷的中国图案，是南洋重新上色的中国。
III

一条完整的产业链

娘惹瓷不是在一个地方完成的。它是三地协作的产物：

景德镇
烧白瓷按南洋订单，烧好白胎或单色釉
广州
上粉彩广州画工按南洋要求加彩、加金
南洋
使用运回槟城、马六甲、新加坡，供峇峇娘惹家庭使用

有些更讲究的订单，会从南洋寄图样到广州，指定画什么纹饰、用什么颜色。这种"定制瓷器"，在当时就是奢侈品——一套完整的娘惹瓷餐具，可能比一栋房子还贵。

今天你在拍卖行看到的娘惹瓷，很多都是那个时代的"订制品"，而不是量产商品。

IV

它为什么今天很贵

娘惹瓷在今天越来越受追捧，原因有三：

一、存世量少。它的生产时间很短，主要集中 19 世纪中到 20 世纪初。之后战乱、社会变迁，加上瓷器本身易碎，完整成套的已经不多。

二、有文化身份。它不是普通的中国外销瓷，而是南洋土生华人身份的象征。买它，不只是买一件瓷器，而是买一段南洋史。

三、颜色特别。那种粉红配孔雀绿的浓艳，在其他任何瓷器门类里都找不到。对收藏家来说，这是一种独一无二的审美。

最近二十年，新加坡、马来西亚、香港的拍卖会上，精品娘惹瓷的价格一直在涨。一套完整的"凤穿牡丹"餐具，价格可以是普通清晚期粉彩的五到十倍。

娘惹瓷的特别之处，不在于工艺多高超。

而在于它是"混血"的——中国的胎、景德镇的窑、广州的彩、南洋的品味。

它不属于任何一个单一的文化，它属于两个文化相遇的地方。

而那个地方，就是我们脚下的南洋。

Walk into a Peranakan shophouse in Penang or Malacca and you will see a whole dining table set with famille rose porcelain — phoenixes, peonies, reds and greens, so vivid it looks like a bride's dress.

This is Nyonya ware.

Not fired in Jingdezhen. Not entirely painted in China either. It is a type of porcelain invented by Nanyang Chinese themselves — white blanks bought from Jingdezhen, coloured in Guangdong, then shipped back to Nanyang for use.

That process is more complicated than you might think.

I

Who "Nyonya" Are

"Nyonya" is a transliteration of the Malay word Nyonya — a female descendant of early Chinese migrants who married local Malay women. The male equivalent is Baba.

This community began forming as early as the fifteenth century — four hundred years before the British arrived in Malaya.

They were neither purely Chinese nor purely Malay. They had their own language, dress, food, and aesthetics. And Nyonya ware is their aesthetics in material form.

❦ ❦ ❦
II

Why Famille Rose

Chinese porcelain comes in many types — blue-and-white, celadon, monochrome, wucai. But Nyonya ware uses almost only one: famille rose.

Why?

Because famille rose is the most vivid.

Famille rose is an overglaze enamel technique capable of soft reds, pinks, yellows, and greens. It is not limited to blue-and-white, nor as plain as monochromes. For Peranakan families living in the tropics, who loved strong colour, famille rose was the natural choice.

They favoured two colours above all: pink and peacock green. Pink for celebration, peacock green for prosperity. The combination is the signature of Nyonya ware.

The motifs are also fixed: phoenix, peony, eight treasures, bats, butterflies. All traditional Chinese auspicious patterns — but the palette and layout are no longer Central Plains. Fuller. Brighter. Louder.

Nyonya ware is China recoloured in the tropics.
III

A Three-City Process

Nyonya ware was not made in one place. It was the product of three cities working together:

Jingdezhen
FiringWhite blanks or monochrome-glazed bodies fired to Nanyang order
Guangzhou
EnamellingCanton painters apply famille rose enamels and gilding to Nanyang spec
Nanyang
UseShipped back to Penang, Malacca, Singapore — for Peranakan households

For more demanding orders, patterns were sent from Nanyang to Guangzhou specifying exactly which motif and which colour. This kind of custom porcelain was luxury goods at the time — a full set of Nyonya ware could cost more than a house.

Much of what you see at auction today are those original custom commissions, not mass-market products.

IV

Why It Costs So Much Today

Nyonya ware has become increasingly sought after for three reasons:

First, scarcity. Its production window was short — mainly mid-nineteenth to early twentieth century. War, social change, and the fragility of porcelain mean that complete sets are rare.

Second, cultural identity. It is not ordinary Chinese export porcelain — it is the symbol of Peranakan identity. Buying it means buying a piece of Nanyang history.

Third, the colour. That pink-and-peacock-green combination cannot be found in any other porcelain category. For collectors, it is a unique aesthetic.

Over the past two decades, prices for fine Nyonya ware at Singapore, Malaysian, and Hong Kong auctions have risen steadily. A complete "phoenix and peony" dinner service can fetch five to ten times the price of ordinary late-Qing famille rose.

Nyonya ware's uniqueness is not in superior craftsmanship.

It is in its hybridity — a Chinese body, a Jingdezhen kiln, a Guangzhou enamel, and a Nanyang taste.

It does not belong to any single culture. It belongs to the place where two cultures met.

And that place is Nanyang, where we live.
