唐代瓷器为什么突然变多？

Why did Tang ceramics suddenly multiply?

一场叛乱，改变了中国瓷器史的走向

A rebellion that changed the course of Chinese porcelain

618 — 907
2026-03-032026-03-03 · 4 分钟阅读4 min read · 古董圈编辑部 · 未经授权禁止转载Gudong Editorial · No reposting without permission

唐代之前，中国人吃饭用陶器、漆器、金银器，瓷器并不是主角。唐代之后，瓷器突然成了中国人日常生活的必需品，而且是最大宗的商品之一。

这个转折是怎么发生的？

答案藏在一场叛乱里。

I

安史之乱，打乱了一切

755 年，安禄山在范阳起兵。此后八年，唐朝陷入内战。长安失守，玄宗逃往四川，半个中国变成战场。

北方大乱，人口大量南迁。

更重要的是：经济重心开始从北方向南方转移。

战争摧毁了北方的农田与手工业，而南方相对安定，人口、资金、技术一起涌向长江流域。中国的经济版图，从此改写。

瓷器产业的命运，也跟着改写。

II

南方的窑口起来了

南方本来就有瓷土。战乱之后，北方的工匠南下，带来了技术和资本。南方窑口，一下子起来了。

最典型的是长沙窑。

长沙窑在今天的湖南长沙铜官镇，兴起于 8 世纪末到 9 世纪。它的特别之处在于：它做的是外销瓷。

长沙窑的瓷器上有阿拉伯文、有椰枣纹、有胡人形象。这些纹饰明显是为中东、波斯市场定制的。也就是说，长沙窑从诞生起，就不是为中国人做瓷器，而是为外国人做。

同时代的越窑（浙江）、邢窑（河北），也进入了大规模生产。越窑做青瓷，邢窑做白瓷，"南青北白"，一南一北，构成唐代瓷器的主干。

战乱把北方人赶到了南方，也把瓷器产业从奢侈品变成了大宗商品。
III

海运打开了销路

唐代之前，中国的对外贸易主要走陆路，丝绸之路从长安向西，穿过中亚到波斯、罗马。

安史之乱后，陆路被切断。唐朝转向海路。

从广州、扬州、明州（宁波）出发，商船经东南亚、印度，抵达波斯湾。这条"海上丝绸之路"，比陆路运输量更大、成本更低。

瓷器虽然重、易碎，但海运相对平稳，只要包装得当，完全可以承担远洋运输。于是，瓷器成了海上贸易的大宗商品。

1998 年，印尼海域发现了一艘唐代沉船"黑石号"。船上载着六万多件瓷器，绝大部分来自长沙窑。这一船瓷器的目的地，是波斯湾。

这条航线，比郑和下西洋早六百年。

IV

瓷器第一次成了"日用"

唐代之前，普通人用的碗盘是陶器，贵族用的是金银器。瓷器贵，介于两者之间。

唐代之后，随着产量上升、成本下降，瓷器逐渐进入普通百姓的日常生活。到了宋代，瓷器已经是中国最普遍的日用品之一。

这个从"奢侈品"到"日用品"的转变，起点就在安史之乱之后。

今天我们看唐代瓷器，会觉得它比宋代粗、比明清笨。但要记住：它是中国瓷器第一次大规模进入寻常百姓家的见证。

一件长沙窑的小碗，放在博物馆的展柜里，看起来平平无奇。

但它是 1200 年前，一位湖南工匠为了出口到波斯而做的。

瓷器从奢侈品变成商品，从中国走向世界，就是从这一代工匠开始的。

而这一切的起点，是一场把半个中国卷入战火的叛乱。

Before the Tang dynasty, Chinese people ate from pottery, lacquerware, and gold and silver vessels. Porcelain was not the main character. After the Tang, porcelain suddenly became a daily necessity — and one of China's largest export commodities.

How did that shift happen?

The answer lies inside a rebellion.

I

The An Lushan Rebellion Broke Everything

In 755, An Lushan raised troops at Fanyang. For eight years, the Tang was engulfed in civil war. Chang'an fell, the emperor fled to Sichuan, and half of China became a battlefield.

The north was in chaos. Populations moved south in huge numbers.

More importantly: the economic centre began shifting from north to south.

War destroyed northern farms and workshops, while the south remained relatively stable. People, capital, and skills poured into the Yangtze basin. China's economic map was rewritten.

The fate of porcelain was rewritten too.

II

Southern Kilns Took Off

The south had clay all along. After the war, northern craftsmen moved south, bringing skills and capital. Southern kilns took off almost overnight.

The clearest example is Changsha ware.

The Changsha kilns, in today's Tongguan town near Changsha in Hunan, emerged in the late eighth and ninth centuries. What made them unusual: they made export porcelain.

Changsha pieces carry Arabic script, date-palm motifs, and images of Central Asian figures. These decorations were clearly customised for Middle Eastern and Persian markets. From birth, Changsha ware was made not for Chinese buyers, but for foreign ones.

The Yue kilns (Zhejiang) and Xing kilns (Hebei) entered mass production at the same time. Yue made celadon, Xing made white ware — "southern green, northern white" formed the two poles of Tang porcelain.

War pushed northerners south — and turned porcelain from luxury into mass commodity.
III

Sea Routes Opened the Market

Before the Tang, China's foreign trade ran mostly overland. The Silk Road led west from Chang'an, through Central Asia to Persia and Rome.

After the An Lushan Rebellion, the land route was cut. The Tang turned to the sea.

From Guangzhou, Yangzhou, and Mingzhou (Ningbo), merchant ships crossed Southeast Asia and India to reach the Persian Gulf. This "Maritime Silk Road" carried more volume at lower cost than the overland route.

Porcelain is heavy and fragile, but sea transport is relatively steady. With proper packing, it could travel across oceans. Porcelain became a bulk commodity of maritime trade.

In 1998, a Tang shipwreck was found off Indonesia — the Belitung wreck. It carried more than 60,000 pieces of porcelain, mostly from Changsha. Its destination was the Persian Gulf.

That route predates Zheng He's voyages by six centuries.

IV

Porcelain Became "Everyday"

Before the Tang, ordinary people ate from pottery; nobles ate from gold and silver. Porcelain sat in between — expensive but not aristocratic.

After the Tang, as production rose and costs fell, porcelain entered daily life for ordinary households. By the Song, porcelain was one of the most common everyday items in China.

The shift from "luxury" to "daily necessity" started right after the An Lushan Rebellion.

Today, Tang porcelain can look coarse next to Song or Ming-Qing wares. But remember: it is the first witness to porcelain entering ordinary Chinese homes at scale.

A small Changsha bowl in a museum case looks unremarkable.

But it was made 1,200 years ago by a Hunan craftsman for export to Persia.

Porcelain's transformation from luxury to commodity, from China to the world, began with that generation.

And the starting point of it all was a rebellion that set half of China on fire.
